A ₹240 Cr manufacturing business. 32 years old. Profitable. Family-run.

The patriarch had built it from a single shed. His most competent child, his daughter, took over at 34.

Her vision was different from his.

She wanted to invest in automation. He had built the business on labour relationships. She wanted to exit a declining product line. He had launched it 18 years ago.

These weren't unreasonable disagreements. They were the right questions for a 32-year-old business to ask.

The problem was where they got asked.

In Monday morning operations meetings, with senior managers in the room, her father would interrupt her. Sometimes correct her. Sometimes overrule her in front of people who reported to her.

He didn't mean to undermine her. He had run those meetings for 32 years. Old habit, not bad intent.

Within six months, her authority had quietly evaporated.

Her resignation was drafted.

Then she made one structural change.

She didn't fight for control. She didn't ask her father to step back. She didn't have the difficult conversation.

She moved the disagreement.

She established a monthly board meeting where her father participated as Chairman. Reserved matters were defined. He could challenge anything in that room.

In operations meetings, only one voice spoke now. Hers.

It took nine months for her father to fully accept the structure. He slipped occasionally. She redirected him each time. The senior team watched the shift.

By month 12, the company had a different rhythm.

The disagreement wasn't the problem. The location was.

Most family business successions fail not because the next generation is unprepared. They fail because the previous generation cannot find a different room to disagree in.

If you cannot solve the people problem, solve the architecture problem.

That is what governance is actually for.
