Twice a year, for seven days, Bill Gates disappears.

No phone calls. No meetings. No visitors. He goes to a cabin in a cedar forest in the Pacific Northwest, alone, with stacks of papers written by Microsoft employees pitching ideas, and a caretaker who drops off two meals a day. He calls it Think Week.

It started small in the 1980s, quiet visits to his grandmother's house to read in peace. Over the decades it became this, seven days, twice a year, completely cut off, while running one of the most valuable companies on earth.

In May 1995, during one of these weeks, he wrote a memo called The Internet Tidal Wave. Microsoft had been slow to take the internet seriously. That memo changed the company's entire posture toward it, and the shift it set in motion helped produce Internet Explorer.

Here is what I notice most about that story. Gates did not schedule Think Week to relax. He scheduled it because he understood something most of us never act on. The best thinking does not happen between meetings. It happens when there are no meetings at all.

Most founders and executives fill every open hour on the calendar, because an empty hour feels like waste. So the calendar stays full, decisions stay reactive, and the only thinking that gets done is thinking about what is already on fire.

You do not need a cabin or seven days. You need one afternoon, undisturbed, with no agenda except your own business, at a distance long enough to see the shape of it instead of just the next task. Most promoters have never once done this on purpose.

The time on your calendar with nothing on it is not empty. It is the only time genuinely being spent on the business instead of in it.
