Two Indian business families, both worth thousands of crores, both facing the same moment. The founder was gone, and the next generation had to decide who controlled what.

One family split the business quietly. A documented settlement, values agreed, each branch got what was theirs, and the group carried on, still on decent terms.

The other family is still in court. Years in, arguing in front of judges over a trust that was never built to survive disagreement.

Same wealth. Same starting point. One difference decided which way it went.

It was never how much everyone loved each other. Every family has old wounds and unequal expectations. The difference was whether anyone had written down, while things were calm, how a disagreement would actually get resolved.

Roughly 79 percent of India's GDP sits inside family-run businesses. Only about 30 percent have a documented succession plan. And even where a plan exists on paper, a well-known industry survey found that while 72 percent of companies said they had one, only 27 percent believed it would hold up when actually tested.

That gap, between having a plan on paper and having a family that will honour it under real pressure, is where most of these fights are born. A family constitution does not remove conflict. Every family has some. It decides in advance whose voice counts, how disputes get resolved, and what happens if people cannot agree, so the fight happens in a family council with a facilitator instead of in open court.

By the time the first real disagreement arrives, it is already too late to write the rules. They should have been written on a calm Sunday, years before anyone needed them.

The cost of that document is a few lakhs and some uncomfortable conversations. The cost of not having it is years, crores in legal fees, and sometimes a family that never fully speaks again.
