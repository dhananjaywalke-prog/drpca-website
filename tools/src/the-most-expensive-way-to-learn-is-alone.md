Twenty years of watching young professionals and first-generation founders start out has taught me something uncomfortable. The most expensive mistakes of a career are made in the first few years, and almost none of them feel like mistakes while they are being made.

The wrong niche, chosen because it was nearest. The wrong clients, kept because saying no felt risky. Underpricing that took a decade to undo. A partnership signed on chemistry alone. None of these announce themselves. At the start of a career, the real danger is not what you do not know. It is what you do not know you do not know.

There is one reliable defence, and it is borrowed scar tissue. A mentor is not motivation or networking. A mentor is compressed time, someone whose decade of expensive lessons you can absorb in conversations, so you pay for fewer of them yourself.

The research agrees. A long-term study at Sun Microsystems found mentees promoted five times more often than those without mentors. A separate survey found 76 percent of people believe mentors matter, yet only 37 percent have one. Everyone believes it. Few act on it. Mostly because they wait to be picked, or chase the most famous person in the room.

Both are errors. The right mentor is rarely the most successful person you can reach. It is someone a few steps ahead on the road you actually want, close enough to remember the terrain, willing to tell you what stings, living a whole life you would want, not just an income. And it is usually mentors, plural. One for craft, one for money, one for judgment.

As for how, never ask anyone to be your mentor. It burdens them with a title. Ask one specific question. Act on the answer. Go back and show them what you did. Advice acted upon is the only currency mentors accept, and the relationship builds itself around it.

Hard work decides how fast you travel. Mentors decide whether the road leads anywhere.
