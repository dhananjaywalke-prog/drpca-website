Valuation is as much an art as it is a science. When methodologies and inputs yield different results, assigning weights to reconcile these differences is a cornerstone of professional judgment. Whether the conclusion is a single value or a range depends on the consistency of results and the purpose of the valuation. Let’s explore how scenarios of consistent and inconsistent outcomes can shape the valuation process.

## Scenario 1: Consistent Results Across Methods

In cases where multiple valuation approaches (e.g., income, market, and cost) produce closely aligned results after adjustments, the process becomes more streamlined. For example:
- An asset’s valuation using the **income approach** (DCF) aligns well with the **market approach** (comparable transactions).

Here, weights can still be applied to prioritize the method offering the most reliable data or the best alignment with the purpose of valuation. For instance:
- In highly liquid markets, **market data** may be given higher weight.

The result is often a **single value**, representing a reconciled point estimate derived from weighted methods. Factors such as **data reliability, market behavior, and the valuation’s purpose** significantly influence the weighting.

## Scenario 2: Inconsistent Results Across Methods

When valuation approaches diverge due to differences in assumptions, data quality, or methodology, the challenge intensifies. Consider this example:
- The **market approach** suggests a higher value for a property due to recent comparable sales, while the **income approach** reflects a lower value based on declining rental income.

In such cases, weights are assigned to reflect the **relevance and reliability** of each method. For instance:
- A higher weight might be assigned to the **income approach** when valuing an income-producing asset in a declining rental market.

The conclusion may take the form of:
- A **range**, when discrepancies highlight significant uncertainty.
- A **single value**, when one method clearly dominates in reliability and relevance.

## Scenario 3: Limited or Subjective Data

When market comparables are scarce or assumptions are subjective, common in valuing illiquid or unique assets like rare art or startups, valuation relies heavily on professional judgment. For example:
- Valuing an early-stage startup with uncertain cash flows might prioritize the **cost approach** due to its robust assumptions.

In these cases, the conclusion is often a **range**, reflecting the inherent uncertainty and variability in the data. The valuer must disclose limitations in the inputs and methodologies used, ensuring transparency.

## Scenario 4: Multiple Purposes for Valuation

When the same asset is valued for different purposes (e.g., taxation vs. financial reporting), the weighting approach may shift. For instance:
- Assigning **higher weight** to the **market approach** for transactional purposes.
- Prioritizing the **income approach** for financial reporting.

This flexibility allows the valuer to tailor conclusions to the intended use, resulting in either:
- A **single value**, or
- A **range**, depending on the use case.

## Conclusion

The assignment of weights in valuation ensures that conclusions are driven by the most **relevant and reliable data** tailored to the asset, market, and purpose. Valuers should observe that:

1. **Consistency in results** often leads to a **single value**, while **inconsistency or uncertainty** results in a **range**.
2. **Documenting the rationale** for weights is critical to maintain transparency and comply with IVS standards.
3. Professional judgment is key to understanding divergent results and selecting the final value.

By assigning weights thoughtfully and transparently, valuers enhance the credibility of their conclusions and uphold the highest professional standards.
