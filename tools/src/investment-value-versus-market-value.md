A niche consumer brand, around 100 crore of revenue, healthy margins, growing steadily. A careful DCF on its own cash flows supported a value near 350 crore. Three and a half times revenue. Defensible, documented, honest.

Then a large FMCG player looked at the same company, and their internal number crossed 1,400 crore.

Neither valuation was wrong. They were answering different questions.

The standalone value asked what these cash flows are worth as they stand, with this company's own distribution reaching perhaps forty thousand outlets, its own marketing budget, its own constraints. The strategic buyer asked something else. What happens to this brand inside our machine.

Their machine had eight lakh outlets, already built, already paid for. Pushing one more brand through it costs almost nothing at the margin. Revenue that would take the founder a decade of capital and struggle, the acquirer could reach in three years by adding a line to a distributor order form. He was not paying fourteen times the founder's revenue. He was paying about three times the revenue the brand would do in his hands, at margins fattened by infrastructure the target never built.

Valuation standards have a name for this, investment value, the worth of an asset to a specific owner, as distinct from its market value to a generic one. The gap is not hype. It is arithmetic, and it exists only when the buyer's infrastructure is already paid for. A buyer who must build the distribution first has no such math, which is why the same brand attracts 350 from five bidders and 1,400 from exactly one.

The founder's mistake is asking only what the market multiple is. The sharper question, before any sale process, is who owns the machine my product multiplies inside, and what am I worth to him specifically.

Your business does not have one value. It has a different value in every buyer's hands, and the whole game is finding the hands.
