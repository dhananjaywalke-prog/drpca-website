In 1988, a physicist stood up at a conference in Poland with a strange idea. Catch invisible particles from deep space by burying light sensors in the Antarctic ice.

He said later he gave that talk "to see if anyone would laugh me off the stage."

Nobody laughed. Nobody rushed in either. The early team was four people. Money was always short, and his own university kept lending funds to keep it alive.

Earlier this month, at 82, Francis Halzen won the Nobel Prize in Physics for it.

Persistence is the obvious lesson. What stayed with me was something he said about himself. He called himself a reluctant leader. He said he tried to get out of running the project many times, and it never worked.

So he did something many founders never do. He accepted what he was not good at. He hired a project director, Jim Yeck, a talented manager, and let him build the detector. Halzen stayed with the science. The detector, a cubic kilometre of sensors under the South Pole, was finished in 2010, ahead of schedule.

Three years later, it found what Halzen had been chasing since 1988.

I see the opposite in many family businesses. The founder is brilliant with the idea, the customer, the deal. He also insists on running purchase, accounts, HR and every vendor call. The business grows to the size of his diary and stops there.

He thinks admitting a limit is a weakness. Halzen's story says it is how the work gets finished.

Try this one thing this weekend. Write down every job you personally hold in your business. Mark the ones someone else would do better than you, not just cheaper. Pick one. Hand it over within 90 days, fully, with the authority that goes with it.

Vision gets you on the stage. Knowing your limits gets the thing built.
