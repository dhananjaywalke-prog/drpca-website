A ₹40 crore related-party lease was on the agenda, item six of nine, scheduled for three minutes.

The company was renting a warehouse from an entity owned by the promoter's family. The lease had been in place for years and simply came up for renewal. The board pack called it routine, same rent, same terms, nothing to discuss. Most of the room was ready to nod it through.

One independent director asked a simple question. Where is the valuation report showing this rent is at arm's length.

There wasn't one. The lease had been priced the same way for years, rolled forward each time without anyone checking it against the market.

The item was deferred. An independent valuation was commissioned. It showed the company was paying about 22 percent above the market rate for comparable warehouse space in the same location, a gap worth several crore a year.

The rent was renegotiated before the lease was signed.

Here is what almost let this slip through. A related-party transaction is not dangerous because it is with family. Plenty of related-party deals are perfectly fair. It is dangerous because it is familiar, and familiarity is what stops a board from asking the one question that protects the company. Is this price what an unrelated party would pay.

The rules already require the answer to be documented, a valuation placed before the audit committee before approval. In practice, on a transaction that has existed for years, that document quietly stops being asked for, because nobody wants to imply the family arrangement needs checking.

It is not about mistrust. Honest pricing drifts from the market over time, and nobody notices drift from the inside.

The most expensive related-party transactions are never the new ones. They are the old ones nobody has re-priced in years.
